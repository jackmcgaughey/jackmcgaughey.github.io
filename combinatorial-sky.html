Plate I.

The Catalan Constellations

One number, wearing different skies. Each card below is a single object drawn four ways — as a mountain path, a chord diagram, a bracket word, and a binary tree — and every card is a different object entirely. The miracle is that all four censuses agree, for every n, forever.

order n
Fig. 1 — the four-fold census Top-left: a Dyck path, n steps up and n down, never dipping below the horizon. Top-right: the same word's matched pairs as non-crossing chords on a circle of 2n stars. Below: the bracket word itself, and the binary tree it folds into. The count is C(n) = (2n choose n)/(n+1) — Stanley's book lists 214 more disguises, but four felt like enough for one plate.
Plate II.

Ramsey's Hexagon — a playable theorem

Six stars, fifteen possible lines. You and the Archivist take turns claiming them — yours gold, the Archivist's rose. Whoever completes a triangle in their own colour has marred the chart and loses. Because R(3,3) = 6, the sky refuses to allow a draw: someone must lose. Try not to let it be you.

Your move. Claim any line.

The Archivist plays cautiously: it will never finish its own triangle while a safe line exists, and it favours lines that leave it the fewest future traps. It can still be beaten — and it will absolutely let you hang yourself.

you 0archivist 0games 0
Fig. 2 — the inevitability engine This is the game of Sim (Simmons, 1969). Colour the 15 edges of K₆ in two colours however you like — a monochromatic triangle always appears; that is exactly the statement R(3,3) = 6. Misère play (triangle = loss) makes the theorem something you can lose to, which is the most intimate way to understand a theorem.
Plate III.

The Sierpiński Nebula

Pascal's triangle, photographed through a modular filter. Each star is a binomial coefficient; it shines only if C(n,k) ≢ 0 (mod m), tinted by its residue. At prime moduli the dust organises itself into perfect self-similar voids — Lucas' theorem, visible to the naked eye.

modulus m 2 rows 128
Fig. 3 — dust and voids For prime p, Lucas says C(n,k) mod p is the digit-wise product of base-p digits — so a void opens wherever a digit of k exceeds the matching digit of n. At m = 2 you get the Sierpiński gasket; at composite moduli the patterns interfere like overlapping nebulae. Slide and see.
Plate IV.

The de Bruijn Zodiac

A ring of 2k stars, bright for 1 and faint for 0, arranged so that a window of k consecutive stars shows every k-bit word exactly once per revolution. Nothing repeats, nothing is missed. The tightest possible almanac.

window k
reading —seen 0 / 8
Fig. 4 — the perfect almanac A de Bruijn sequence B(2,k) exists because the de Bruijn graph — vertices are (k−1)-bit words, edges append a bit — has every vertex with in-degree = out-degree = 2, so an Eulerian circuit exists. Walk the circuit, read the edge labels, and the zodiac assembles itself. There are 2^(2^(k−1))/2^k distinct such rings; this plate shows one.
Plate V.

The Partition Clusters

Every way of breaking n into a sum, drawn as a cluster of stars — a Ferrers diagram. Click any cluster and it conjugates: rows become columns, the partition reflects across its diagonal, and a different sum looks back at you. Some clusters are their own reflection. Find them.

n 6 p(n) = —self-conjugate —p(50) = 204 226, for scale
Fig. 5 — reflections Conjugation is the involution that proves theorems for free: it swaps "at most k parts" with "largest part at most k" instantly. The self-conjugate clusters are in bijection with partitions into distinct odd parts — peel them into nested hooks and watch each hook's length come out odd and different. Euler would have loved a touchscreen.
Plate VI.

A Random Sky — Erdős & Rényi, G(n, p)

Twenty-four stars. Each of the 276 possible lines was assigned a secret threshold at the moment this page loaded; the slider is the rising probability p, and a line ignites the instant p passes its threshold. Slide slowly. Watch dust become clusters, clusters become a giant, the giant swallow the sky. The phase transition is not a metaphor — it is the actual shape of the curve under your finger.

Both events are sharp: a whisper below the threshold almost nothing, a whisper above almost certainty. You met this graph in CO 342. It did not mention that you'd get to hold the dial.
